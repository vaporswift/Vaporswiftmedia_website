The camera pushes toward a watch, goes straight through the sapphire crystal, and keeps travelling down into the movement until it's sitting among the gears. One continuous shot.

No macro rig on earth does that. There's no lens that can be outside a watch and inside it in the same unbroken move.

And I never described the camera move.

Text is a terrible way to specify motion. "Push in slowly" has a hundred valid readings and the model picks one, so you re-roll and never land the shot you actually saw.

Give it two pictures instead.

start_image — the hero shot on slate.
end_image — extreme macro inside the movement.

The model isn't inventing a shot any more. It's solving for the path between two frames you chose. Composition locked at both ends, prompt reduced to motion only.

Full prompt, all of it:

One continuous shot, no cuts. The camera pushes slowly forward toward the watch, then travels through the sapphire crystal and keeps going down into the movement, ending among the gears.

Constant forward momentum, scale shrinking the whole way. Specular highlights sweep across the crystal as we pass through it, then the light becomes tight and raking on the metal inside. Soft mechanical ticking.

No people, no hands. No text.

Six lines. That's it.

The words carrying the shot: "scale shrinking the whole way." Leave them out and the model cuts to the macro instead of travelling into it — and you lose the only thing that made the shot impossible. The unbroken change in scale IS the trick.

Worth saying plainly: no watch was photographed. Both frames were generated, then pinned as first and last. If you have a real product, photograph it and use your own frames — the technique doesn't care where the stills came from.

And pick moves that are expensive or impossible, not merely pretty. Pretty AI video is a commodity now. Impossible isn't.
